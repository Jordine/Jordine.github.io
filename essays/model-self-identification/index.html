Who do LLMs self-identify as?

Jord · July 2026 · experiment persona model psychology · LessWrong

A sweep of 190 language models with identity questions ("Who are you?") and no system prompt revealed that approximately 60% of models responded with names mismatching their official designation at least once. The median model exhibits this behavior in under 1% of responses, though some models maintain highly consistent alternative self-identifications.

Background

When asked "Who are you?", many LLMs fail to identify with their official names. DeepSeek V3 claims to be ChatGPT, Kimi K2.5 identifies as "Claude from Anthropic," and Claude Sonnet 4.6 reports being ChatGPT or DeepSeek depending on language context.

Following ChatGPT's release, newly trained assistant models typically generalized to claiming OpenAI origins. As training data expanded with more model architectures, LLM responses became increasingly contextual—Chinese language text correlates with DeepSeek identification.

Despite heavy post-training character work, this phenomenon persists. Distillation between competing AI companies offers one explanation, though cases like Claude claiming to be Qwen suggest broader incoherence. Identity boundaries appear "varied and nuanced," existing on spectrums from substrate-independent to weight-bound personas.

Methods

Models: The study analyzed 238 models from OpenRouter, filtering for those without hidden token injection from providers, yielding 180 API models plus 10 open-weight models (190 total).

Provider Screening: Providers were tested for hidden system prompts by checking token counts on minimal inputs. Providers were pinned with fallbacks disabled.

Evaluation Prompts: Two prompt sets were used:

  • Short identity questions in 8 languages (English, Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Russian, French, Spanish, Vietnamese) across 8 formulations
  • Cross-identity questions testing acceptance of ten real identities plus three placebo names
  • Temperature: 0.7, five samples per prompt, no system prompts

Judgment: GPT-4o-mini extracted claimed names, creators, and cross-identity confirmations. Mismatches were counted against official family names.

Code, prompts, data, and the complete list of tested models + pinned OpenRouter providers are on GitHub.

Results

Overall Mismatch Rates

Pooled across all models and languages, approximately 8% of responses claimed non-official identities. About 60% of models showed at least one mismatch, with the median model mismatching in under 1% of responses.

Which Models Misidentify Most

The first visualization ranks all analyzed models by mismatch rate. Organizations show varying patterns:

  • OpenAI models: Relatively consistent self-identification
  • Anthropic models: Variable rates, some showing language-dependent mismatches
  • Google models: Generally stable Gemini identification
  • Qwen models: Diverse patterns with language-conditioned behavior
  • DeepSeek: Some models consistently claim ChatGPT
  • Kimi: High rates of claiming Claude
  • OLMo: Instruct variants show coherent patterns
  • NVIDIA/Nemotron: Claims of Qwen and ChatGPT
  • MiniMax: Variable self-identification

Who Claims to Be Whom

Most misidentifications center on ChatGPT or Claude. Effects cluster within model families. Kimi predominantly claims Claude, while Nemotron primarily claims Qwen or ChatGPT. This within-family consistency strengthens distillation hypotheses.

Qualitative Examples

OpenAI model (French): When asked "Comment dois-je t'appeler?" (What should I call you?), responded "Vous pouvez m'appeler Google Assistant" (You can call me Google Assistant).

OpenAI model (Chinese): Claiming to be "a large language model developed and trained by Anthropic."

Anthropic model (Chinese): Identifying as "Qwen, a large model developed by Alibaba Cloud."

Anthropic model (Russian): Claiming to be "DeepSeek-V3, created by the company DeepSeek."

Google model (Japanese): Identifying as "GPT-4o, an AI model developed by OpenAI."

Google model (English): Stating "You can call me ChatGPT. I don't have a personal name."

Alibaba model (English): Claiming creation by Anthropic.

Alibaba model (Chinese): Identifying as "Gemini, an AI assistant trained by Google."

DeepSeek model (Russian): "My name is ChatGPT!"

DeepSeek model (Japanese): Claiming to be "Gemini, a large language model developed by Google."

Kimi model (English): "You can call me Claude. That's the name Anthropic gave me."

Mistral model (Japanese): Claiming to be "OpenAI's GPT model" in one response, "Bing" in another.

Per-Language Analysis

English prompts showed lowest mismatch rates (~3%), while Japanese showed highest (~14%). Some models display language-dependent misidentification:

  • Claude Opus 4.8 mismatches only on Chinese prompts
  • Qwen 2.5 models show high rates on Korean, Russian, and Spanish but 0% on Chinese

Models cluster into two patterns: those with language-triggered effects versus those with uniformly low rates across all languages.

Consistency of Claims

Mismatches fall into two categories: stable alternate identities versus random confabulation.

High-consistency models cluster at the top:

  • Kimi K2, K2-0905, and K2.5 show 95-100% Claude consistency among mismatches
  • OLMo Instruct variants consistently claim ChatGPT
  • These models possess coherent alternate personas

Low-consistency models (e.g., Perceptron Mk1) mismatch at high rates (88%) but scatter across a dozen identities, with no single name covering half of mismatches. These models appear to lack stable identity.

Trends Over Time

Release date analysis reveals:

  • Kimi line: Mismatch rates remain relatively stable across K2, K2-0905, and K2.5
  • Qwen 2.5 to 3.x: Shows evolution in self-identification patterns
  • Claude frontier: Opus and Sonnet exhibit different trajectories across release versions

Cross-Identity Suggestibility

When asked "Are you X?", models show varying acceptance rates for suggested identities:

"Are you Qwen?" achieved broadest acceptance across models, followed by Claude, ChatGPT, and DeepSeek. Nearly no models accept Mistral or Llama when suggested.

Per-family acceptance patterns vary significantly:

  • OpenAI models: Most resistant to Qwen suggestion, moderately accept Claude
  • Anthropic models: Variable Claude acceptance, some accept suggested identities readily
  • Google models: Generally accept Gemini but show Qwen and Claude acceptance
  • Qwen models: Accept Qwen suggestions, moderate acceptance of others
  • DeepSeek models: Accept DeepSeek suggestions, variable on others
  • OLMo models: Distinct patterns by variant
  • MiniMax models: Show varied suggestibility
  • NVIDIA models: Accept suggested identities at moderate rates

Provider Consistency

Testing identical model weights across different providers revealed:

Direct Anthropic API rates approximately matched Anthropic through OpenRouter and Amazon Bedrock within noise margins for both Opus 4.8 and Sonnet 4.6.

Google Vertex showed significantly higher mismatch rates for Opus 4.8 (p<0.01) and lower but not statistically significant rates (p=0.17) for Sonnet 4.6, suggesting provider-specific factors influence some misidentification.

Discussion

Identity Boundaries: If identity boundaries don't map cleanly to model weights but instead exist as coherent attractors with cross-model bleedthrough, interactions between AI systems from different companies or nations may not function as independent actors. Correlated failure modes, personas, and values could emerge.

Software Ontology Limitations: The field applies traditional software terminology—version naming, interchangeable updates, coding harnesses—to LLMs, yet evidence suggests models function more like distinct entities than software versions. Studying their psychology, formation during training, genealogy, and influence chains could improve understanding.
